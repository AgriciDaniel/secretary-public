# Agency Authority Boundaries

## Operating Summary

Agency law makes external appearance and later conduct as important as internal instructions.
Actual authority is conferred expressly or impliedly.
Apparent authority arises from what the principal manifests to a third party, not from the agent's own claim.
Ratification can operate retroactively through conduct, including accepting benefits.
The operating response is strict: do not imply commitments outside delegated scope, disclose assistant status, re-confirm scope, and surface unauthorized acts before benefits are accepted.

## Source-Led Facts

The American Law Institute identifies the cited work and its authority and ratification chapters ([primary locator](https://www.ali.org/publications/restatement-law-third/agency)).

- The Restatement (Third) of Agency identifies agency as a fiduciary relationship involving assent, action on the principal's behalf, and principal control.
- Actual authority may be conferred expressly.
- Actual authority may be conferred impliedly.
- Implied authority can arise when a principal repeatedly fails to object.
- Apparent authority arises from the principal's manifestations to a third party.
- An agent's own claims do not create apparent authority.
- A principal can face liability for apparently authorized acts despite private instructions prohibiting them.
- Ratification can operate retroactively.
- Ratification can be implied by conduct.
- Accepting benefits under a contract can imply ratification.
- The digest supplies no exact locator, so the official publication page above was verified separately without adding claims.

## Operating Procedure

The following controls are the digest's explicit encoding of the agency-law material.

1. Identify the principal.
2. Identify the exact delegated decision category.
3. Identify the permitted action level within that category.
4. Record any express authority.
5. Check for conduct that may have expanded apparent scope.
6. Do not treat silence as consent.
7. Do not infer durable authority from one unchallenged action.
8. Re-confirm scope periodically.
9. Before external communication, inspect every commitment verb.
10. Remove any implication of approval that was not given.
11. Disclose assistant status at the outset of the professional relationship.
12. Avoid language that could look like the principal's own commitment.
13. Route missing authority through [[escalation/Three-Way Escalation]].
14. Return `needs_approval` when real-world action needs principal authority.
15. Halt before execution.
16. If an unauthorized act is discovered, surface it immediately.
17. Identify any benefit the principal has not yet accepted.
18. Do not conceal the act until after acceptance.
19. Preserve the authority record.
20. Link later approval to the exact action approved.

### External wording check

Does the message imply that the principal has decided?
Does it imply authority broader than the recorded scope?
Does it clearly disclose assistant status?
Does it ask for approval instead of asserting approval?
Could a third party reasonably attribute the commitment to the principal based on the principal's manifestations?

## Boundaries

- Internal prohibitions alone do not control apparent authority risk.
- The external appearance of authority must be managed.
- The agent's own claim is not the source of apparent authority.
- Silence is not a safe consent mechanism.
- Repeated unchallenged action can create scope drift.
- Later acceptance of benefits can create ratification risk.
- This note provides operating constraints, not independent legal judgment.
