# CEO Time-Use Baseline

## Operating Summary

Use the CEO time-use study as a baseline for auditing a principal's calendar, not as a target the principal must imitate.
The digest records a reported 27-CEO observational sample and related time-use
figures, but its primary is a paywalled business magazine article and the
repository only holds secondary reproductions. Treat the values as contested
context, not validated performance targets.

## Source-Led Facts

The reported average work week was 62.5 hours (https://hbr.org).
The reported weekday average was 9.7 hours (https://hbr.org).
The CEOs worked on 79 percent of weekend days (https://hbr.org).
They worked on 70 percent of vacation days (https://hbr.org).
Meetings occupied 72 percent of recorded time (https://hbr.org).
Alone time occupied 28 percent (https://hbr.org).
Of alone time, 59 percent came in blocks of one hour or less (https://hbr.org).
Seventy-five percent of the calendar was pre-scheduled (https://hbr.org).
Twenty-five percent was spontaneous (https://hbr.org).
Forty-three percent of time advanced the CEO's own agenda (https://hbr.org).
Thirty-six percent was reactive (https://hbr.org).
Forty-two percent of meetings were one-to-one (https://hbr.org).
Internal interactions accounted for 70 percent and external interactions for 30 percent (https://hbr.org).
Named practices included a written personal agenda (https://hbr.org).
Named practices included a quarterly calendar audit against that agenda (https://hbr.org).
Named practices included directly challenging default meeting length (https://hbr.org).
One CEO's reported rule was to cut requested meeting time in half (https://hbr.org).
The source warns that excessive gatekeeping can make a leader appear imperious, self-important, or out of touch (https://hbr.org).

## Operating Procedure

The following procedure is synthesis from the study, not a published protocol.

1. Ask the principal to maintain a written personal agenda.
2. Treat that agenda as the comparison set for calendar review.
3. Separate agenda-advancing time from reactive time.
4. Separate meetings from alone time.
5. Identify fragmented alone-time blocks.
6. Separate pre-scheduled time from spontaneous time.
7. Separate internal from external interaction.
8. Identify one-to-one meetings as their own class.
9. Review the distribution once per quarter.
10. Show the distribution as observed data, not as a moral score.
11. Flag material drift from the principal's written agenda.
12. Link irreversible or authority-sensitive items to [[judgment/Reversibility as the Master Gate]].
13. Link deadline signals to [[judgment/Urgency and Importance]] before ranking them.
14. Link principal-designated reporting needs to [[escalation/CCIR]].
15. Challenge default meeting duration only as a nominated adjustment.
16. Do not cancel, decline, or shorten external commitments without authority.
17. Distinguish a recommendation from an approved calendar change.
18. Preserve dissent when calendar compression would hide a material concern.
19. Name any data period that is incomplete.
20. Do not compare the principal with the sample without stating the sample boundary.

## Boundaries

The sample is small and restricted to 27 CEOs.
The digest does not establish that the averages are optimal.
The digest does not supply a universal balance between meetings and alone time.
The digest does not supply an ideal percentage for agenda-advancing time.
The study does not authorize the secretary to reject access requests.
The source explicitly warns against excessive gatekeeping.
Use [[gaps/No Primary Gatekeeping Question Set]] when asked for a sourced qualification questionnaire.
Use [[duties/Paper Flow and Calendar Horizons]] for planning horizons, not this note.
Use [[failure-modes/Private Office Failure Modes]] when diary capture or over-gatekeeping appears.
Do not attribute 25 or 50 minute defaults from this source.
Use [[gaps/Meeting Trim Attribution Correction]] for that separate issue.
