# Briefing and Debriefing

## Operating Summary

Debriefing is a high-value routine with meta-analytic support in this family.

A meta-analysis of forty-six samples reports that debriefs "improve
effectiveness over a control group by approximately 25%".

A large surgical study reports an "18% reduction in annual mortality" in
facilities enrolled in a team training program requiring briefings and
debriefings.

That second result is a bundle and cannot isolate debriefing.

Treat the meta-analytic effect as the load-bearing finding and the mortality
result as directionally consistent support.

The operating synthesis for staff work is that a structured review after the
fact is a valuable addition to preparation. The cited evidence does not compare
debriefing with additional instruction before work.

Build the debrief in as a routine, not only as a response to failure.

## Source-Led Facts

Tannenbaum and Cerasoli meta-analyse team and individual debriefs
([PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23516804/)).

The published abstract records findings from forty-six samples.

The abstract states that debriefs "improve effectiveness over a control group by
approximately 25%".

The abstract records that effects were similar for teams and for individuals.

It records that effects were similar across simulated and real settings.

It records that effects were similar for medical and nonmedical samples.

Neily and colleagues report on a medical team training program
([PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20959579/)).

The published abstract records an "18% reduction in annual mortality" in
facilities in the training program.

The abstract records a smaller decrease among facilities not yet trained.

The abstract states the program required briefings and debriefings in the
operating room and included checklists.

The design is retrospective with a contemporaneous control.

The intervention is a bundle, so debriefing is not isolated.

## Operating Procedure

The following procedure is synthesis directed by the digest.

1. Brief before work that has a decision at the end of it.
2. State the objective, the constraints, and what would change the plan.
3. Name explicitly what would count as the plan going wrong.
4. Debrief after the outcome is known, on a fixed cadence, not only after failure.
5. Compare what happened against what the brief predicted.
6. Record which predictions were wrong and why.
7. Record whether any dissent raised earlier changed the outcome.
8. Feed that record to [[failure-modes/Psychological Safety and Silence]].
9. Classify what went wrong using [[judgment/Threat and Error Management]].
10. Keep the debrief record even when nothing went wrong.
11. A debrief that only runs after failure teaches that debriefs mean blame.

For clinical-style handoffs specifically, use [[I-PASS Briefing Schema]], which
carries measured outcome evidence of its own.

## Boundaries

This note does not establish that briefing alone causes better outcomes.

The mortality result comes from a bundled intervention.

Do not attribute it to debriefing by itself.

This note does not import a checklist product.

It does not establish an optimal debrief length or cadence.

The stored extracts do not supply either.

Record `no data` if asked.

This note does not claim the effect transfers to solo administrative work
without qualification.

The meta-analysis covers individuals as well as teams, which supports the
transfer, but the sampled settings are not secretarial.

Do not present a 25 percent improvement as a promise to the principal.
