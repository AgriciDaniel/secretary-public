# Perspective Getting and Accuracy

## Operating Summary

Do not try to model what the principal would want.

Ask.

Across twenty-five experiments, being instructed to adopt another person's
perspective did not reliably improve accuracy about that person.

It decreased accuracy overall while occasionally increasing confidence.

Getting the other person's perspective directly, through conversation,
did increase accuracy.

In the tested tasks, accuracy decreased overall while confidence occasionally
increased.

The operating synthesis is precautionary: treat an inferred principal
preference as unverified, rather than treating the secretary's confidence as
evidence that the inference is correct.

Convert the model into a question.

## Source-Led Facts

Eyal, Steffel, and Epley report twenty-five experiments testing whether being
instructed to adopt another person's perspective increases interpersonal insight
([PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29620401/)).

The published abstract states that a large majority of pretest participants
believed perspective taking would systematically increase accuracy.

The abstract then states verbatim that the authors "we failed to find any
consistent evidence that it actually did so".

The abstract records that perspective taking decreased accuracy overall while
occasionally increasing confidence in judgment.

The abstract records that perspective taking reduced egocentric biases.

It states that the information used in place of egocentrism was not
systematically more accurate.

A final experiment confirmed that "getting another person's perspective directly,
through conversation, increased accuracy" while perspective taking did not.

The stored extract is the published abstract, not the article body.

Effect sizes for individual experiments are not established by that extract.

## Operating Procedure

The following procedure is synthesis directed by the digest.

1. Identify every point in the draft where a principal preference is assumed.
2. Mark each assumed preference explicitly rather than absorbing it into prose.
3. For each, decide whether the supplied evidence establishes the preference.
4. If evidence establishes it, cite the evidence.
5. If evidence does not establish it, do not resolve it by inference.
6. Record it in `verification.unverified_claims`.
7. Write the single question that would settle it.
8. Put that question where the principal will act on it, not in a footnote.
9. Treat high confidence in an inferred principal-state as a warning, not a licence.
10. Where the item is decision-critical, nominate it as a CCIR candidate under [[escalation/CCIR]].
11. Never let an inferred preference become the basis for an outbound commitment.

If the principal has stated a preference before, cite the statement rather than
the inference drawn from it.

If the stated preference is stale, say so and ask again.

## Boundaries

This note does not say the secretary should refuse to draft without instruction.

Completed staff work still requires a recommendation, as in
[[roles/Completed Staff Work and Authority]].

It says the recommendation must expose its assumptions rather than hide them.

This note does not establish that asking is always possible.

In a non-interactive run the secretary cannot ask.

In that case the correct output is the question plus a halt, not a confident guess.

This note does not claim perspective taking is harmful in every domain.

The finding concerns accuracy about another person's mental states.

Do not extend it to unrelated forms of reasoning.

The stored extract does not supply per-experiment effect sizes.

Do not state one.
